Data Preparation

Why This Page Exists

Every figure on the analysis pages comes from one table. This page documents how that table was built: what we pulled, what we kept, what we removed, and what we repaired. Anyone reading our conclusions should be able to judge how much weight the evidence can carry.

Data Source

The panel is built from the WRDS jobs_2026 dataset supplied for Module 3: 504,208 postings across 134 columns, distributed as twenty-one Parquet parts with a manifest, per-column coverage report and checksums.

Modules 1 and 2 used the MET Employability Career Match API. We moved to the supplied dataset in Module 3 and measured the two sources against each other on our exact scope before deciding:

Field MET API panel Module 3 dataset
Postings in scope 6,199 7,074
US state recorded 28% 92%
Remote status recorded 48% 65%
Skills listed 93% 100%
Education level 59%, parsed from text 69%, a coded field
Annual salary usable 19% 25%
Years of experience 66%, parsed from text 56%, a numeric field
Occupation code (SOC) 98% 33%

The switch is not an improvement on every axis. Occupational coding is substantially sparser and experience is recorded on fewer postings, though as a number rather than a figure we extracted with a regular expression. Geography was the single largest limitation of the earlier panel and it largely disappears, the skill fields arrive split into named tools, specialisations, transferable skills and certifications, and education and experience stop being our estimates and become the provider’s.

Industry and Career Scope

Industry code NAICS 2022 — 523, Securities, Commodity Contracts, and Other Financial Investments and Related Activities
Career pathway Business Intelligence / Data Analyst
Date window Postings published on or after 1 September 2025
Build date 22 September 2026

The date window keeps the analysis current: the source spans postings back to 2014, and hiring requirements from a decade ago describe a market our target user is not applying into. In practice the window costs little — only 192 in-scope postings predate it.

Why this industry, and why a three-digit code

Module 1 anchored the project to NAICS 5221, Depository Credit Intermediation. That code returned 59 postings in total, too few to support compensation or skill-gap analysis, and we re-anchored to NAICS 523 after measuring the neighbouring codes. NAICS 523 accounts for roughly two-thirds of all analytics postings within Finance and Insurance, so it is where the market actually is.

The assignment asks for four- or six-digit codes where possible. The earlier source carried no four-digit granularity beneath 523; this dataset does expose NAICS4 through NAICS6, and those columns are retained in the panel for finer analysis, but 523 remains the scope boundary so that the project stays comparable across modules.

Role Definition

The assignment allows title keywords, SOC or O*NET mappings, skills logic, or a combination, provided the choice is explained. We use a combination, and this dataset makes the case stronger than before: occupation codes are present on only a third of postings, so an occupation-only filter would discard two postings in three unseen.

A posting is kept when either condition holds:

  • its SOC occupation matches one of: data scientist, database architect, statistician, mathematician, operations research, market research analyst, financial and investment analyst, credit analyst, management analyst
  • or its title contains one of: analyst, analytics, data, business intelligence, BI, reporting, SQL, data science, machine learning

Information-security roles are then removed. They match the word “analyst” but belong to a different career pathway, and including them would distort the skill profile the product reports.

Postings without a publication date are the largest single exclusion. They cannot be placed inside the date window, and keeping them would mean reporting a twelve-month market using rows of unknown age.

Cleaning Decisions

Cleaning stays conservative. Where a value is implausible we blank that field rather than guess a replacement, and where a whole posting is questionable we keep it and record the gap. Deleting rows narrows the sample; blanking one field costs only that field.

The rules below were written for Module 2 by Katie Bowles and carried over unchanged; only the column names differ, and the salary handling is extended because this dataset quotes pay in several periods.

Duplicates. Deduplicated on the posting identifier. None remained.

Rows missing essentials. A posting is dropped if it has no identifier, title, employer or publication date. After the date filter, none failed this test.

Dates. Parsed to a single format. Where an expiry date preceded the publication date the expiry would be blanked, since one of the two must be wrong and we cannot tell which; no postings were affected.

Salary. The provider’s SALARY column turned out to be a free-text summary whose value is an empty placeholder on most rows, so it is kept for reference but never used as a figure. The usable numbers are the structured range, and that range is quoted in different periods — 1,751 annual, 315 hourly, 18 monthly. Each is validated against bounds appropriate to its own period rather than a single annual rule, which had been silently deleting hourly rates as “implausible”. Reversed ranges are swapped, zeros treated as missing, and 20 rows were cleared as out of range across all periods.

Hourly and monthly rates are kept in their own columns and are not converted to annual figures, because the conversion requires an assumption about hours worked that the postings do not supply. ANNUAL_SALARY_MID — the midpoint of the annual range — is the field used for salary analysis, and it is populated on 1,741 postings.

Experience and education. Taken directly from the provider’s coded fields. Unlike the earlier panel these are not our estimates, so no parsing decisions were needed.

Skills. Arrive as arrays and are stored as semicolon-separated strings across four fields: all skills, named software, specialised skills and transferable skills, plus certifications. Names are not yet normalised, so casing and synonyms remain distinct; that normalisation belongs to the skill-gap work, where it has an analytical purpose.

Remote status. Reported exactly as the source provides it, and never inferred. A posting with no remote label is an absence of information, not evidence of an office requirement.

Location. Text fields trimmed of stray whitespace. This dataset carries no coordinates, so the latitude and longitude check from the earlier pipeline does not apply; geography is carried by state, city and metropolitan area.

Types. Every column is cast explicitly — dates, booleans, numbers and categories — so later analysis cannot silently treat a number as text.

Data Quality and Limitations

Coverage in the panel of 7,074 postings:

Field Populated Share
Skills 7,074 100%
Named software skills 7,074 100%
US state 6,532 92%
Minimum education level 4,913 69%
Remote status 4,568 65%
Minimum years of experience 3,941 56%
Occupation code (SOC 2021) 2,357 33%
Annual salary usable 1,741 25%

Three limitations shape how the analysis pages should be read.

Salary is disclosed on one posting in four. Employers who publish pay ranges are not a random sample — pay-transparency law applies in some states and not others — so the disclosed group likely skews toward those jurisdictions and toward larger employers. Every salary figure in this project describes that subset, not the market.

Occupation codes are sparse. Only a third of postings carry a SOC occupation. Any breakdown by occupation describes the coded third, and comparisons between occupations are ratios within that subset rather than counts of the whole market.

Experience is recorded on just over half of postings. It is a reliable number where present, but absence is common and should not be read as “no experience required”.

Against that, geography and skills are close to complete, which means the location analysis and the skill-gap work — the parts the product most depends on — rest on much firmer ground than the compensation analysis does.

Reproducing This Step

  1. Download the Module 3 dataset into data/raw/m3_dataset/
  2. pip install -r requirements.txt
  3. python notebooks/19_build_panel_wrds.py — scope filter, role filter, cleaning
  4. python notebooks/21_fix_salary.py — period-aware salary validation
  5. python notebooks/22_data_dictionary_m3.py — data dictionary

All work runs on the course AWS EC2 environment.
